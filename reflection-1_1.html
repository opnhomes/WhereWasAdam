The Gap · Part I · Identity

The Question We Need to be Asking

On who society says we should be, who God made us to be, and what’s lost when we confuse the two.

“God created mankind in his image; in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them.” Genesis 1:27

Who are we — really?

Not who society says we’re supposed to be. Not the roles we fill or the titles we carry — husband, father, provider, employee. Those are real. They’re not nothing. But they’re not the foundation. If we build our identity on a role, the day that role changes — we lose the job, the marriage struggles, the kids grow up and leave — we lose ourselves with it.

The question of identity is the first question God ever asks a man, and it doesn’t go away until it’s answered.

It’s older than we think

Before Adam did anything — before he named the animals, before he was given a task, before Eve — he was made in the image of God. Identity came before assignment. That order matters. We are not what we do. We are who He made us to be, and only after that does the doing follow.

Then Genesis 3 happens. The serpent comes for Eve, and Adam — present, watching, silent — says nothing. And when God comes walking in the garden, He doesn’t ask Adam “what did you do?” first. He asks something more basic:

“Where are you?” Genesis 3:9

Not a request for coordinates. A question about presence. About a man who was made in the image of God and, in the moment that mattered, went missing while standing right there.

That’s the wound this whole series is named for. But it’s not where the story ends.

The Second Adam answers the question the first one couldn’t

Scripture doesn’t leave Adam’s failure as the final word on manhood. St. Paul calls Christ the last Adam (1 Corinthians 15:45) — the one who succeeds exactly where the first Adam failed. Where Adam was silent, Christ speaks: “not my will, but yours be done” (Luke 22:42). Where the serpent told Adam he could be like God (Genesis 3:5) and lose who he already was, Christ — who actually was God — “did not regard equality with God as something to be grasped” (Philippians 2:6), and lived from that identity instead of reaching for a false one. Where Adam hid from God, Christ walked toward the Cross in full view.

This matters for identity because it means our model isn’t a myth or a standard we’re chasing alone. It’s a Person. And more than a model — a source. The grace to actually become the men we’re made to be doesn’t come from willpower. It comes from union with Christ himself.

We were made to stand in the gap

There’s a line the prophet Ezekiel gives that names exactly what this series is asking of us: God says He “sought for a man among them who should build up the wall and stand in the gap before me for the land, that I should not destroy it — but I found none” (Ezekiel 22:30, paraphrased). That’s the ancient shape of the calling. A gap opens — in a wall, in a family, in a friendship, in a parish — and God looks for a man willing to stand in it. Adam had that post in Eden and left it empty. We have it now, wherever we are.

So — who are we?

Not our job titles. Not our worst failures, or our best years. We are men made in the image of God, given a post to stand in, capable of the same silence Adam fell into — and capable, by grace, of the same obedience Christ offered instead. That’s the identity underneath every other role in our lives. Everything else in this series — our interior life, marriage, fatherhood, parish, friendship, and work — is just this same question, asked again in a different room.

Week 1 Challenge · Identity

Who am I, really?

This week has two parts:

Part 1 — Name your false foundation. Find time to sit in front of the Blessed Sacrament. Ask our Lord to show you the one role, title, or achievement your sense of self collapses without — job, salary, athletic past, reputation. Just name it honestly, and write it down.

Part 2 — Pray Genesis 1:27 aloud once a day this week. Not as a memory exercise — as a declaration. “God created me in his image.” Let the identity precede the task, even for sixty seconds, before the day begins.

The goal isn’t performance. It’s reorientation. Identity before assignment — practiced, not just proclaimed.
