The One Who Turned Back

On thankfulness, and the difference between entitlement and giving thanks.

“In all circumstances give thanks, for this is the will of God for you in Christ Jesus.”1 Thessalonians 5:18
Tip: use the ← → keys

Part 1 of 6Begin

It’s easy to keep a list of what’s missing: the job we didn’t get, the marriage that’s harder than we hoped, the kids who won’t listen, the body that’s starting to slow down. Gratitude doesn’t ask us to pretend the list isn’t real. It asks us to notice we’ve been keeping only one list.

Part 2 of 6Ten were cleansed.

On the road to Jerusalem, ten lepers call out to Jesus from a distance, and he tells them to go show themselves to the priests. “As they were going they were cleansed” (Luke 17:14). All ten. Then one of them, realizing he had been healed, “returned, glorifying God in a loud voice” (Luke 17:15), and fell at the feet of Jesus and thanked him. He was a Samaritan, a foreigner. Jesus asks the question we should hear in our own lives: “Were not ten cleansed? Where are the other nine? Has none but this foreigner returned to give thanks to God?” (Luke 17:17–18). Nine got what they came for. One got something more. Jesus tells him, “Stand up and go; your faith has saved you” (Luke 17:19). All ten were healed. Only one was saved, because only one turned back.

Part 3 of 6Adam turned a gift into a grievance.

Remember how Adam answered God in the Garden: “The woman whom you put here with me — she gave me fruit from the tree” (Genesis 3:12). Look at what he did there. The woman, the gift God had given him to end his loneliness, became the evidence in his defense. He took the best thing he’d been given and used it as a grievance. That’s what ingratitude does. It rewrites every gift as someone else’s fault.

Part 4 of 6The New Adam gave thanks.

On the night before he died, Jesus took bread and a cup, and the Gospels use the same word each time: he “gave thanks” (Luke 22:17, 19; Matthew 26:27). The night he was betrayed, he gave thanks. The Church’s name for what we receive at Mass, the Eucharist, is the Greek word for thanksgiving. Every Sunday we stand and hear the priest say, “Let us give thanks to the Lord our God,” and we answer, “It is right and just.” Right and just. Gratitude is the truth about who God is and who we are. It isn’t a mood. It’s accuracy. And it’s why we can be thankful for the Mass itself. Nothing we could earn or buy is offered there. Christ gives us his own Body and Blood, the gift that makes every other gift make sense, and he asks only that we come back and receive it. The nine went on their way. The Mass is where we turn back, every week, and say thank you.

Part 5 of 6Give thanks in all circumstances.

St. Paul writes, “In all circumstances give thanks, for this is the will of God for you in Christ Jesus” (1 Thessalonians 5:18). Not for everything. In everything. In the hard marriage, the tight season, the unanswered prayer, there is still something to turn back and thank him for. Looking for it is what changes us.

Part 6 of 6This Week’s Challenge

Bonus Week · Gratitude

Turn back to God, and give thanks from the heart.

There is no assignment this week. After several weeks of challenges, this week asks for one thing: turn back to God and thank him. Set aside a few quiet minutes each day, and in your own words, not from a list, thank him for what he has given you. Thank him for the ordinary gifts, and for the hard places where you can’t yet see the gift. Then, at the Thanksgiving table and at Mass, when the priest says “Let us give thanks to the Lord our God,” mean every word.
