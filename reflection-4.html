The Gap · Part IV · Fatherhood

The Weight of the Word

On strength that protects instead of controls, and the fathers we’re actually meant to become.

“Tend the flock of God in your midst... Do not lord it over those assigned to you, but be examples to the flock.” 1 Peter 5:2–3

Few words carry more weight than father. It carries everything — protection, presence, blessing, legacy. And for too many of us, shame. Because somewhere between the man we intended to be and the man we became, a gap opened up. And our children are living in it.

Strength was never meant to dominate.

St. Peter gives fathers — and every man given anyone to lead — a specific instruction: tend the flock, don’t lord it over those in your care. “Do not lord it over those assigned to you, but be examples to the flock” (1 Peter 5:3). St. Paul gives fathers the same correction inside the home: “Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up with the training and instruction of the Lord” (Ephesians 6:4). That’s a real correction, because it’s easy to think fatherhood is mostly about authority: the rules we set, the house we run, the final word we get. But a shepherd’s strength doesn’t exist to control the flock. It exists to stand between the flock and the wolf. If our strength is only ever felt as control, we’ve built something closer to a cage than a home. If it’s felt as protection — a place everyone counting on us feels safer because we’re there — we’re closer to what the word actually means.

St. Maximilian Kolbe gave his life so a father could keep his.

In 1941, at Auschwitz, a prisoner named Franciszek Gajowniczek was condemned to death by starvation as punishment for another man’s escape. He cried out for his wife, his children. A Franciscan priest, Fr. Maximilian Kolbe, stepped forward and asked to die in his place. We call every priest “Father,” and that day Kolbe showed what the word means. He laid down his life so that another man’s family would not lose theirs. It was total self-giving love, spent on a family that wasn’t his own. That’s the same post named back in Ezekiel — a man standing in the gap so it doesn’t destroy what’s on the other side of it.

Padre Pio never stopped showing up.

For decades, St. Pio of Pietrelcina — Padre Pio — heard confessions for ten, twelve, sometimes fifteen hours a day, carrying the wounds of the stigmata in his own body the entire time. Padre means father, and that is what he was to the people who knelt in his confessional: a spiritual father who heard them, absolved them in Christ’s name, and sent them back to their families new. He was relentlessly, physically present to whoever showed up needing him — even when it cost him everything he had left. Fatherhood asks for a similar version of that same endurance: the version of us that’s still present after the tenth bedtime story, the third argument this week, the day we had nothing left to give and gave it anyway.

A father’s heart works quietly, mostly in the shadows.

Pope Francis wrote an entire letter — Patris Corde, “With a Father’s Heart” — reflecting on St. Joseph, who never says a single word in Scripture. Joseph protects, provides, and obeys, entirely in silence, entirely without credit. Francis calls this a fatherhood lived “in the shadows” — not absent, but quietly, faithfully present without needing to be seen. That’s worth sitting with, because it cuts against how easy it is to perform fatherhood loudly while going quiet exactly where Joseph never did — in the unseen, unglamorous, unrewarded work no one’s watching us do.

For the man who isn’t a father

If you don’t have children, this week isn’t a pass. Fatherhood is bigger than biology. Kolbe and Padre Pio were priests, spiritual fathers to people who weren’t their own, and St. Paul says the same of himself: “I became your father in Christ Jesus through the gospel” (1 Corinthians 4:15). Spiritual fatherhood isn’t only for priests. It’s the nephew who needs a steady man, the younger man at work or in the parish who has no one to learn from, the kid down the street, the friend who’s going under. Whether God is calling you to marriage, to the priesthood, or to a single life given to Him, you’ll be asked to stand between someone and the wolf. So learn now what strength is for. Pray for whoever God is placing under your care, and for the children you may one day have.

So — what kind of strength are we bringing home?

Not the kind that needs to win the argument, or be obeyed, or be thanked. The kind St. Maximilian Kolbe gave, the kind Padre Pio gave, the kind St. Joseph gave without a single recorded word: strength spent protecting, not controlling. Where has our strength been serving us instead of them?

Week 4 Challenge · Fatherhood

Is my strength a shelter or a cage?

This week has two parts:

Part 1 — Ask, and listen. Spend time in Adoration and ask: where has my strength been serving me instead of them? Then ask one person who depends on you — a child, your wife, a young man you look out for — one question: “When do you feel safest with me, and when don’t you?” Don’t defend. Don’t explain. Listen, thank them, and write down what you heard. If you’re not a father, ask our Lord who He is placing in your life to protect and look out for.

Part 2 — Be Joseph, in the shadows. Each day this week, do one thing for someone in your care that no one will see and no one will thank you for. Then each night, if you have children, make the sign of the cross on each child’s forehead and pray a short blessing over them by name. If you don’t, pray for the people God has put under your care.

The goal isn’t a grand gesture. It’s the quiet, ordinary strength that stays, week after week, between the people we love and whatever is coming for them.
