The Gap  ·  Part IV  ·  Fatherhood

Week 4  —  Nov 1–7

The Weight of the Word

On strength that protects instead of controls, and the fathers we're actually meant to become.

"Shepherd the flock of God that is among you... not domineering over those in your charge, but being examples." 1 Peter 5:2–3

There is no word in the human language more loaded with expectation than father. It carries everything — protection, presence, blessing, legacy. And for too many of us, shame. Because somewhere between the man we intended to be and the man we became, a gap opened up. And our children are living in it.

Strength was never meant to dominate

St. Peter gives fathers — and every man given anyone to lead — a specific instruction: shepherd, don't dominate. Not domineering over those in our charge, but being examples (1 Peter 5:2–3). That's a real correction, because it's easy to think fatherhood is mostly about authority: the rules we set, the house we run, the final word we get. But a shepherd's strength doesn't exist to control the flock. It exists to stand between the flock and the wolf. If our strength is only ever felt as control, we've built something closer to a cage than a home. If it's felt as protection — a place everyone counting on us feels safer because we're there — we're closer to what the word actually means.

Kolbe gave his life so a father could keep his

In 1941, at Auschwitz, a prisoner named Franciszek Gajowniczek was condemned to death by starvation as punishment for another man's escape. He cried out for his wife, his children. A Franciscan priest named Maximilian Kolbe stepped forward and asked to die in his place — not because Gajowniczek was his own son, but because he was somebody's father. Kolbe, as a priest, understood this: that real strength is what you're willing to lay down so someone else's family doesn't lose theirs. That's the same post named back in Ezekiel — a man standing in the gap so it doesn't destroy what's on the other side of it.

Padre Pio never stopped showing up

For decades, Padre Pio heard confessions for ten, twelve, sometimes fifteen hours a day, carrying the wounds of the stigmata in his own body the entire time. He wasn't a biological father either, but generations of men and women called him one, because he was relentlessly, physically present to whoever showed up needing him — even when it cost him everything he had left. Fatherhood asks for a smaller version of that same endurance: not fifteen-hour days, but the version of us that's still present after the tenth bedtime story, the third argument this week, the day we had nothing left to give and gave it anyway.

A father's heart works quietly, mostly in the shadows

Pope Francis wrote an entire letter — Patris Corde, "With a Father's Heart" — reflecting on St. Joseph, who never says a single word in Scripture. Joseph protects, provides, and obeys, entirely in silence, entirely without credit. Francis calls this a fatherhood lived "in the shadows" — not absent, but quietly, faithfully present without needing to be seen. That's worth sitting with, because it cuts against how easy it is to perform fatherhood loudly while going quiet exactly where Joseph never did — in the unseen, unglamorous, unrewarded work no one's watching us do.

So — what kind of strength are we bringing home?

Not the kind that needs to win the argument, or be obeyed, or be thanked. The kind Maximilian Kolbe gave, the kind Padre Pio gave, the kind St. Joseph gave without a single recorded word: strength spent protecting, not controlling. Where has our strength been serving us instead of them?
