The Gap · Part VI · Friendship

Men Without Brothers

On the friends we need, the friends we’ve let drift, and the men we’re meant to carry.

“Iron is sharpened by iron; one person sharpens another.” Proverbs 27:17

Don’t we all desire authentic friendship, rooted in authentic masculinity? Not the easy kind. We have plenty of that: colleagues, neighbors, teammates, guys we text about the game. What we’re hungry for is a man who knows where we’re actually struggling, who shares the standards we’re trying to live by and holds us to them when we start to slide, and who would tell us the truth even when it costs him something. We want a brother like that, and we want to be one. For a lot of us, though, that place is still empty. We’ve built full lives and quietly accepted being alone in them.

It is not good for the man to be alone.

The first thing God calls “not good” in all of creation isn’t sin. It’s a man by himself: “It is not good for the man to be alone” (Genesis 2:18). God answers that with Eve, and we spent Week 3 on what that gift asks of us. But notice what God is naming here. Before there was any sin to fix, there was something in a man that was never meant to be carried alone. Marriage answers part of it. It doesn’t answer all of it, and it was never meant to. Our wives aren’t supposed to be our only confidant, our only accountability, the only person who ever tells us the truth. A man was made for brothers too, and that’s where this week turns. What happens when men refuse that is written just two chapters later. Cain, with his brother’s blood still on the ground, is asked, “Where is your brother Abel?” He answers, “I do not know. Am I my brother’s keeper?” (Genesis 4:9). It’s the same evasion Adam gave in the Garden, only now aimed at a brother instead of God. And we already know the answer. Yes. We are.

Four men and a roof.

In Mark’s Gospel, a paralyzed man can’t get to Jesus on his own. So four men carry him. When the crowd blocks the door, they climb up and open the roof and lower him down in front of Jesus. And then Mark writes one of the most important lines in the Gospel for men like us: “When Jesus saw their faith, he said to the paralytic, ‘Child, your sins are forgiven’” (Mark 2:5). Their faith. The man on the mat didn’t earn it. His friends carried it for him. Most of us are one of those men right now, either on the mat, or on the roof, or sitting in the crowd saying nothing.

Faithful friends are life-saving medicine.

Scripture is not shy about this. “Faithful friends are a sturdy shelter; whoever finds one finds a treasure” (Sirach 6:14). “Faithful friends are life-saving medicine” (Sirach 6:16). And Ecclesiastes: “Woe to the solitary person! … a three-ply cord is not easily broken” (Ecclesiastes 4:10, 12). But Scripture is just as honest about what real friendship costs. “Better is an open rebuke than a love that remains hidden. Trustworthy are the blows of a friend” (Proverbs 27:5–6). A friend who only tells us what we want to hear isn’t much of a shelter. And Proverbs 27:17 puts it plainly: iron sharpens iron. Sharpening means friction. We need men who’ll risk a little of it for our sake, and we need to be that man for someone else.

Pier Giorgio Frassati and the friends who carried him.

Pier Giorgio Frassati, the young Italian layman recently named a saint, was famous for climbing mountains and for serving the poor of Turin, but what stands out is how he did it: with friends. He gathered a close group of young men who prayed together, hiked together, and held each other to something higher. He called them the “Tipi Loschi,” the “shady characters,” with a wink. They weren’t a program. They were brothers who made each other better. When he died at twenty-four, they were the ones carrying his coffin through crowds of the poor he had quietly served. That’s the kind of friendship worth wanting: men who make us holier simply by being around.

No greater love.

Jesus doesn’t describe our relationship to him in terms of rank. He says, “No one has greater love than this, to lay down one’s life for one’s friends… I have called you friends” (John 15:13, 15). The one who shaped our whole idea of manhood calls us friends. If that’s how he treats us, we can’t keep treating the men around us like acquaintances.

Week 6 Challenge · Friendship

Who are my brothers — and whose brother am I?

This week has two parts:

Part 1 — Name three. Write down the names of up to three men you trust, or want to trust, or have let drift. Then reach out to at least one of them with a specific invitation and a specific time: coffee on Thursday, a walk Saturday morning, a phone call tonight. Not “we should get together sometime.” A real plan.

Part 2 — Be one of the four. When you meet, ask one real question — “How are you actually doing?” — and then listen without fixing. Before you part, ask to pray with him right there. Be the man who carries someone’s mat for a while.

And one more thing: this week a group of men will be standing outside a local strip club, praying.

Pray for them, and for the men and women inside.

If your list is empty, ask a priest or the men of your parish how to find one man. Everyone starts somewhere, and the next step is a conversation.
